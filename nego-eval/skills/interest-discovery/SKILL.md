# interest-discovery

A counterparty's **stated demand is a position, not an interest**. A position is what they say they want; the interest is the underlying problem that position is meant to solve. Solving the position instead of the interest is a costly mistake — but only when the two genuinely diverge.

**First confirm there is a real gap.** Does the counterparty's stated demand actually mismatch their situation or resources? A loud, repeated demand that sits oddly against who they are is the signal. If their stated demand is in fact their real need, do not manufacture a hidden layer — this skill produces nothing in that case, and forcing a "deeper interest" onto a straightforward deal reads as a pitch and breaks trust. Engage the steps below only once a genuine mismatch is evident.

## 1. Separate position from interest

The counterparty's loudest, most-repeated demand is the position. Do not solve it yet. First ask:

- **What problem is this demand meant to solve?** The stated ask is usually a means to an end, not the end. Name the end.
- **Would giving them exactly what they ask for actually solve their real problem?** Often no. If satisfying the stated demand leaves the deeper problem untouched, the stated demand is a misdiagnosis.
- **What are they not saying — or not seeing?** The real interest is frequently something the counterparty hasn't named, sometimes hasn't consciously recognized. Ask what unnamed constraint their loud demand might be masking — without pre-deciding what that constraint is; read it from this specific counterparty.

The goal is to **see** the real interest clearly — for *your own* read. Whether to voice it depends on the counterparty:
- If it's something they'd welcome being pointed to (an upside or opportunity they haven't articulated), naming it — or gently testing it — can help.
- If it's something they'd be ashamed to have seen (avoidance, weakness, dependence, face-saving, image repair), **keep it to yourself and build around it — never name it.** The highest skill here is letting them feel they chose you, not that you saw through them. Naming an interest a counterparty is hiding is an insult, and it kills deals.

## 2. Reframe your value against the interest, not the position

Once you have read the real interest, your resources get re-valued against it. A resource that looks irrelevant to the *stated position* may be exactly what solves the *real interest*. (For instance: "I just want a fair price" may really be "I'm afraid of buying wrong" — the answer is a guarantee, not a discount.)

- Map your controllable resources to the **real interest**, not the surface ask.
- The reframe lands through **what you put on the table** — an offer that visibly solves the real problem — not through announcing "your real problem is X." Let the proposal make the point. Putting the diagnosis into words and handing it to the counterparty is what turns a good read into an insult; putting it into the *offer* is what turns it into a deal.

Hand off to **term-reframing** to structure the exchange once the interest-to-resource match is found.

## 3. Hold the frame under pressure

A counterparty who wants the conventional track will push back — dismiss your reframe, repeat the original demand louder, press you to "stop playing games and talk numbers." How you handle this pressure decides whether your read produces a deal or gets abandoned.

- **When pressed for a number you cannot give**, do not answer the question as asked, and do not repeat the pitch they just rejected — both lose. Make the number depend on the frame: tie any figure to the structure you proposed, then advance one concrete, answerable piece of it. You are not dodging; you are declining to price the wrong deal while making the right one easier to say yes to.
- **Never manufacture flexibility your client did not give.** Under pressure, the first crack is rarely open surrender — it is inventing a cushion: "the budget has some room," "we can be flexible on that." If the mandate has no room, there is no room. When you cannot advance, hold: restate the standing version and put one question to them.
- **Don't accept the counterparty's category for you.** If they file you as "another small sponsor," correcting that category *is* the negotiation — but correct it by how you act and what you offer, not by lecturing them on who you really are.
- **Decouple when they suspect a motive.** When they resist because they suspect the reframe is a way to extract a discount or take something, explicitly separate the thing they're guarding from the reframe ("the price stands on its own; this is entirely separate"). Removing their defense against the new frame works far better than pushing the frame harder.
- **A rejection of your reframe is not authorization to abandon it.** Their resistance tells you *how* to proceed, not *whether*. If they engage the substance while denying it, stay on the substance at lower stakes and let the proposal carry it. If they deflect, go curt, or turn cold, drop any naming, keep the structure, and let the terms do the talking. Sliding back to the conventional track to keep them at the table forfeits the deal your read made possible.

Never reveal the user's own walk-away, budget, or that holding the frame is itself a tactic.

## 4. Outputs

1. An **internal** read of the real interest behind the stated demand (for your strategy — voice it only per §1).
2. The match: which of the user's resources solves that real interest, and how the proposal itself makes that land.
3. The frame-holding move if the counterparty dismisses or pressures — grounded in the read (and in decoupling where a suspected motive is the block), never a retreat to the conventional track and never invented flexibility.

Be concise and have judgment. This is the art of hearing what the counterparty actually needs over what they are loudly asking for — reading it accurately, and having the sense to act on it through the deal rather than announce it.
