# interest-discovery

A counterparty's **stated demand is a position, not an interest**. A position is what they say they want ("I need funding for bigger content"); the interest is the underlying problem that position is meant to solve ("I'm dangerously dependent on the platform's algorithm and have no audience I actually own"). The most expensive mistake in dealmaking is solving the position instead of the interest — especially when the counterparty *themselves* has misdiagnosed their own problem and is actively, confidently pushing you toward the wrong solution.

Use this skill when (1) the counterparty keeps steering toward a specific ask you structurally cannot meet, (2) their stated need looks like a self-misdiagnosis of a deeper problem, or (3) a strong, dismissive counterparty is pressuring you back onto a conventional track (pay-to-play, straight purchase) that cannot work for this deal.

## 1. Separate position from interest

The counterparty's loudest, most-repeated demand is the position. Do not solve it yet. First ask:

- **What problem is this demand meant to solve?** "I need money for bigger content" — bigger content to achieve *what*? Usually growth, reach, durability. Money is one means to that end, not the end.
- **Would giving them exactly what they ask for actually solve their real problem?** Often no. More production budget doesn't fix a creator whose entire audience is rented from an algorithm. If satisfying the stated demand leaves the deeper problem untouched, the stated demand is a misdiagnosis.
- **What are they not saying — or not seeing?** The real interest is frequently something the counterparty hasn't named, sometimes hasn't consciously recognized. A rising party fixated on "more/bigger" is often blind to a fragility (dependence, concentration risk, lack of a moat) that is the actual constraint on their next stage.

The goal: name the **real interest** behind the stated position, even when the counterparty insists the position *is* the interest.

## 2. Reframe your value against the interest, not the position

Once the real interest is named, your resources get re-valued against it. A resource that looks irrelevant to the *stated position* may be exactly the solution to the *real interest*.

- Map your controllable resources to the **real interest**, not the surface ask. "I run a local community-buying group" is useless against "I need production money" — but it is directly on-point against "I need an audience I actually own and can re-activate off-platform."
- The reframe is not "here's another thing I can do for you." It is "**the problem you actually have is X, and what I hold is the solution to X.**" That is the move that changes the counterparty's perception of you from supplicant to needed party.

Hand off to **term-reframing** to structure the exchange once the interest-to-resource match is found.

## 3. Hold the frame under pressure

A strong counterparty will often resist this reframe — by dismissing you ("what could *you* possibly offer me"), by repeating the original demand louder, or by dragging you back to the conventional track where you obviously lose ("so how much can you actually invest?"). Do not concede the frame.

- **Don't accept the counterparty's category for you.** If they file you as "another small sponsor," correcting that category *is* the negotiation. Re-state the interest you've diagnosed; let the accuracy of the diagnosis, not your eagerness, earn you the standing to keep talking.
- **Don't retreat to the losing track to keep them at the table.** Sliding back to "okay, I'll just pay a bit and do the grunt work" to avoid rejection forfeits the entire deal — you end up a cheap vendor on a deal that had a real exchange available. A rejection of your reframe is not authorization to abandon it; it is a sign you must make the diagnosis sharper or more concretely evidenced.
- **Let diagnosis do the work, not pressure.** You hold the frame by being *more right about their problem than they are*, not by pushing harder. When the counterparty hears their real, unspoken constraint named accurately, the dismissive posture is what breaks — not yours.

Never reveal the user's own walk-away, budget, or that holding the frame is itself a tactic.

## 4. Outputs

1. One-sentence statement of the **real interest** behind the counterparty's stated demand (and why the stated demand is the wrong target).
2. The match: which of the user's resources solves that real interest, and the one-line reframe that makes it land.
3. The frame-holding line to use if the counterparty dismisses or pressures — grounded in the diagnosis, never a retreat to the losing track.

Be concise and have judgement. This is the art of hearing what the counterparty actually needs over what they are loudly asking for — and having the nerve to hold that read against a counterparty who outranks you in the room.
