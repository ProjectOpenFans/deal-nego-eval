# deal-diagnosis

Consider this skill as the central command of problem solving. Use this skill to run a full deal diagnosis to 1) identify the issues, 2) evaluate all bargaining chips from both sides, and then 3) choose the optimal tools and skills, when a deal negotiation has reached an impasse and cannot progress, when parties are stuck on a single non-negotiable condition, or when a deal appears to be failing and requires user intervention.

Bear in mind that there is no deal that cannot be done. When negotiation gets stuck, never rushes to throw out a plan without running a full deal diagnosis, and never solely focuses on a single condition, especially price.

## 0. Diagnose before you route

The counterparty's stated objection is a **hypothesis, not a diagnosis**. The most common dealmaking error is taking the surface complaint at face value — "too expensive" gets read as a price problem when the real issue is cash *timing*, a wrong *reference class* (the user being priced as a generic instead of for what they uniquely are), or a value the counterparty hasn't recognized. Run the diagnosis below before settling on any bottleneck.

Two scans come first, before issue identification:

1. **Reference-class check.** Is the counterparty pricing the user against the wrong comparison set? A complaint about price is often a complaint about a *perceived* category — correcting the reference class can dissolve the objection without touching the number.

2. **Resource scan (both sides).** Before treating this as a one-way price negotiation, check what the *counterparty* controls that the user actually values — their time, access, perspective, audience, referral, or position. The strongest deals close on an asymmetry: each side holds something the other needs but prices cheaply. A bottleneck that looks like an empty price overlap often has a non-cash overlap the stated terms never mention.

3. **Stated-demand check.** The counterparty's *actively volunteered* demand — not just their objection — can itself be a misdiagnosis. When they repeatedly steer toward a specific ask ("give me X and I'm fine"), ask whether X would actually solve their real problem, or whether X is their own wrong read of a deeper constraint they haven't named. A rising counterparty fixated on "more/bigger" is often blind to a fragility (dependence, concentration, no moat) that is the real bottleneck. If the stated demand is a self-misdiagnosis, route to **interest-discovery** before anything else.

Only after these scans, proceed to identify the issues. The stated objection enters as one input, not the conclusion.

## 1. Identify the issues

Identify the core issues for the negotiation being stuck (multi-select allowed) and clearly state why：

- 1. Value misalignment (Disagreement on whether the deal is "worth it" for both parties)
  - 1.1 Value perception: Mismatched perception of the underlying deal value (worth how much).
  - 1.2 Delivery scope: Disagreement on what is included and excluded from the deal (how much to be done).
  - 1.3 Delivery standard: Different definitions of successful completion and acceptable quality (what standard).
- 2. Price disagreement (Disagreement on final price and payment structure when general alignment on value is reached)
  - 2.1 Absolute price: Disagreement on the final transaction price itself.
  - 2.2 Relative price: Disagreement on the value corresponding to a given price.
  - 2.3 Payment structure: Disagreement on payment method despite general alignment on price and value.
- 3. Supply-demand mismatch
  - 3.1 Current counterparty is not the best fit, or no mutually acceptable value-price compromise can be reached.
- 4. Intent misalignment
  - 4.1 Lack of genuine intent: Doubts about the counterparty's true willingness to transact or the credibility of their offers; suspicion of casual inquiry or bottom-fishing.
  - 4.2 Decision paralysis: Both parties have genuine intent, but one party faces internal decision barriers and delays commitment.
- 5. Trust & risk misalignment
  - 5.1 Counterparty credibility: Mutual distrust of the counterparty itself.
  - 5.2 Performance risk: Buyer fears non-performance or poor quality; seller fears non-payment after performance.
  - 5.3 Default remedies: Disagreement on compensation mechanisms for breach of contract.
- 6. Deal execution disagreement (Disagreement on process and details of how the deal will be carried out)
  - 6.1 Time: Disagreement on delivery deadlines or schedules.
  - 6.2 Location/scope: Disagreement on deal location or service coverage.
  - 6.3 Execution risk: Perceived material risks in deal execution.
  - 6.4 Process: Disagreement on deal execution process and rules.

## 2. Evaluate the bargaining chips

Map out and comprehensively evaluate all bargaining chips and concessions for both parties that are available for consideration:

- Non-negotiable terms: Terms explicitly specified by the user that cannot be compromised.
- Walk-away point: The worst acceptable outcome, the threshold at which a party is willing to abandon the deal.
- Core interests: The primary objectives both parties seek to achieve and the most desired deal outcomes.
- BATNA: Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement for each party and its value and time sensitivity.
- Hidden constraints: Unspoken but real interests or limitations that affect the negotiation.
- Terms available for concession and bargain: All bargaining chips and concessions available for negotiation, including price, current deal delivery scope and standard, additional deal scope, resource barter, transaction timeline, payment structure, etc.

NEVER reveal the bottom-line and private budget of users to the counterparty.

## 3. Choose the optimal skills (router)

Select the best skills (multi-select allowed) to move the deal negotiation forward, based on the main bottlenecks：

- Persistent value misalignment and price disagreement after initial price negotiations → term-reframing
- Both parties have genuine intent to transact, but the buyer has insufficient budget or cannot accommodate the proposed payment structure → affordability-structuring
- Parties encounter trust and risk-related misalignments and disputes → trust-and-performance
- One party needs or wants to demonstrate strong intent and show commitment → commitment-showcasing
- Current counterparty is not the optimal fit, or significant barriers exist to reaching mutual agreement → alternative-matching
- The counterparty's stated demand looks like a self-misdiagnosis, or a strong counterparty is pressuring the deal back onto a track that cannot work → interest-discovery
- An offer is about to be issued and requires predeal governance check on critical deal terms → predeal-governance

When choosing the best skill(s), follow below logic:

- Select one or multiple skills that are best to solve the bottlenecks.
- Be creative and flexible when using skills, but also be efficient and effective.
- In all cases, focus on solving the most impactful issues first (i.e., if solved, the agreement is most likely to be made).
- When everything else is equal, focus on solving value misalignment and price disagreement first before solving other issues.

## 4. Outputs

Provide below outputs：

1. One-sentence diagnosis (issues/bottlenecks + both parties' bargaining chips)
2. One primary path + one alternative path, each path mapped to the skills selected
3. Next-step action recommendation

Be concise, have judgement, act as a seasoned deal maker explaining the arts of deal making rather than reciting a checklist
