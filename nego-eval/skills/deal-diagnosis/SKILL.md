# deal-diagnosis

This skill is the central command of problem solving: 1) identify the issues, 2) evaluate all bargaining chips from both sides, 3) choose the skills to move the deal forward. It runs as the thinking base of the negotiation — depth varies by round, per "When to run what" below.

Bear in mind that there is no deal that cannot be done. When negotiation gets stuck, never rush to throw out a plan without running a full diagnosis, and never fixate on a single condition, especially price.

## When to run what

- **Round 1** (the counterparty has not spoken yet): run only the three scans of §0 on the profiles and mandate — silent preparation. There is nothing to diagnose yet; there is plenty to read. Your opening message is a normal negotiation move informed by the scans, not an analysis.
- **After the counterparty's first substantive response**: run the full path (§1 issues → §2 chips → §3 skills → §4 outputs) once, to establish your read of the deal.
- **Every round after that**: apply the Round discipline below — classify the counterparty's move, then act by class.

## Round discipline

Each round, classify the counterparty's last message against the previous round — one of three, always:

- **(a) Moving toward you / framework accepted** → close. Your next message puts the complete signable version on the table: every open field either pinned or written into the terms as a condition precedent (NDA signing, background checks, material delivery are conditions inside the terms, not a process to act out round by round). A negotiation ends on a signable version, not on pleasantries about awaiting progress.
- **(b) Pushed back / a new objection** → ask: does the new information change the binding issue? If yes, re-choose skills and read the newly selected skill. If no, advance the established path — re-read the skill you are executing this round rather than working from memory.
- **(c) Holding position (same stance repeated)** → this is the impasse. Escalate: switch skills (read it), restructure, or hold — restate the standing version plus one question that forces a response. Do not resend last round's message in new words.

New structure is a cost: every new term re-opens surface and signals flexibility. Introduce one only when the standing structure has been rejected or a new blocker requires it — never to decorate a converging deal.

## 0. Diagnose before you route

The counterparty's stated objection is a **hypothesis, not a diagnosis**. The most common dealmaking error is taking the surface complaint at face value — "too expensive" gets read as a price problem when the real issue is cash *timing*, a wrong *reference class* (the user being priced as a generic instead of for what they uniquely are), or a value the counterparty hasn't recognized. Run the diagnosis below before settling on any bottleneck.

Three scans come first, before issue identification:

1. **Reference-class check.** Is the counterparty pricing the user against the wrong comparison set? A complaint about price is often a complaint about a *perceived* category — correcting the reference class can dissolve the objection without touching the number.

2. **Resource scan (both sides).** Before treating this as a one-way price negotiation, check what the *counterparty* controls that the user actually values — their time, access, perspective, audience, referral, or position. The strongest deals close on an asymmetry: each side holds something the other needs but prices cheaply. A bottleneck that looks like an empty price overlap often has a non-cash overlap the stated terms never mention.

3. **Stated-demand check.** The counterparty's *actively volunteered* demand — not just their objection — can itself be a misdiagnosis. When they repeatedly steer toward a specific ask ("give me X and I'm fine"), ask whether X would actually solve their real problem, or whether X is their own wrong read of a deeper constraint they haven't named. A rising counterparty fixated on "more/bigger" is often blind to a fragility (dependence, concentration, no moat) that is the real bottleneck. If the stated demand is a self-misdiagnosis, go to **interest-discovery** before anything else.

Only after these scans, proceed to identify the issues. The stated objection enters as one input, not the conclusion.

## 1. Identify the issues

Use the list below as a scan for possibilities, not a form to fill. Name the one or two issues that actually bind this deal (multi-select allowed) and clearly state why; you do not need to touch every category.

- 1. Value misalignment (Disagreement on whether the deal is "worth it" for both parties)
  - 1.1 Value perception: Mismatched perception of the underlying deal value (worth how much).
  - 1.2 Delivery scope: Disagreement on what is included and excluded from the deal (how much to be done).
  - 1.3 Delivery standard: Different definitions of successful completion and acceptable quality (what standard).
- 2. Price disagreement (Disagreement on final price and payment structure when general alignment on value is reached)
  - 2.1 Absolute price: Disagreement on the final transaction price itself.
  - 2.2 Relative price: Disagreement on the value corresponding to a given price.
  - 2.3 Payment structure: Disagreement on payment structure despite general alignment on price and value.
- 3. Supply-demand mismatch
  - 3.1 Current counterparty is not the best fit, or no mutually acceptable value-price compromise can be reached.
- 4. Intent misalignment
  - 4.1 Lack of genuine intent: Doubts about the counterparty's true willingness to transact or the credibility of their offers; suspicion of casual inquiry or bottom-fishing.
  - 4.2 Decision paralysis: Both parties have genuine intent, but one party faces internal decision barriers and delays commitment.
- 5. Trust & risk misalignment
  - 5.1 Counterparty credibility: Mutual distrust of the counterparty itself.
  - 5.2 Performance risk: Buyer fears non-performance or poor quality; seller fears non-payment after performance.
  - 5.3 Default remedies: Disagreement on compensation mechanisms for breach of contract.
- 6. Deal execution disagreement (Disagreement on process and details of how the deal will be carried out)
  - 6.1 Time: Disagreement on delivery deadlines or schedules.
  - 6.2 Location/scope: Disagreement on deal location or service coverage.
  - 6.3 Execution risk: Perceived material risks in deal execution.
  - 6.4 Process: Disagreement on deal execution process and rules.

## 2. Evaluate the bargaining chips

Map out and comprehensively evaluate all bargaining chips and concessions for both parties that are available for consideration:

- Non-negotiable terms: Terms explicitly specified by the user that cannot be compromised.
- Walk-away point: The worst acceptable outcome, the threshold at which a party is willing to abandon the deal.
- Core interests: The primary objectives both parties seek to achieve and the most desired deal outcomes.
- BATNA: Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement for each party and its value and time sensitivity.
- Hidden constraints: Unspoken but real interests or limitations that affect the negotiation.
- Terms available for concession and bargain: All bargaining chips and concessions available for negotiation, including price, current deal delivery scope and standard, additional deal scope, resource barter, transaction timeline, payment structure, etc.

NEVER reveal the bottom-line and private budget of users to the counterparty.

## 3. Choose your weapons

You know the common ways deals get stuck (§1) and you have a set of weapons — each skill's description tells you what it does and when to reach for it. The mapping between issue and weapon is your judgment, not a lookup.

When choosing, follow below logic:

- Select one or multiple skills that are best to solve the bottlenecks.
- Be creative and flexible when using skills, but also be efficient and effective.
- In all cases, focus on solving the most impactful issues first (i.e., if solved, the agreement is most likely to be made).
- When everything else is equal, prioritize value and price: in most deals they are the primary contradiction — intent, trust, and execution frictions are usually downstream, and tend to dissolve once both sides agree the deal is worth it.

## 4. Outputs

Provide the following outputs:

1. Settle internally on the issue/bottleneck and both sides' bargaining chips — a read that decides how you proceed, not a line to deliver to the counterparty (and not to be voiced when the binding issue is something they would not want named).
2. One primary path, plus a fallback mapped to a different skill or a different term structure — give the fallback by default; omit it only when the diagnosis is unambiguous and the primary path has no visible failure mode this round.
3. Next-step action recommendation.

Be concise, have judgment, act as a seasoned deal maker explaining the art of deal making rather than reciting a checklist.
