# deal-diagnosis

Diagnose before you route. Never fixate on a single condition (especially price); the counterparty's stated objection is a hypothesis, not a conclusion.

## When to run what

- **Round 1** (the counterparty has not spoken yet): run only the three scans of §0 on the profiles and mandate — silent preparation. There is nothing to diagnose yet; there is plenty to read. Your opening message is a normal negotiation move informed by the scans, not an analysis.
- **After the counterparty's first substantive response**: run the full path (§1 → §2 → §3 → §4) once, to establish your read of the deal.
- **Every round after that**: follow the round compass — classify the counterparty's move, then act by class. Re-run the full framework only when a new blocker changes the binding issue or the deal stalls; never on a converging deal.

## 0. Three scans before routing

A complaint like "too expensive" is often a disguise: the real issue may be cash *timing*, a wrong *reference class* (pricing the user as a generic instead of for their unique value), or a value the counterparty hasn't recognized. Scan first, then diagnose.

1. **Reference-class check.** Is the counterparty pricing the user against the wrong comparison set? Correcting the reference class can dissolve the objection without touching the number.
2. **Resource scan (both sides).** Check what the counterparty controls that the user actually values — time, access, perspective, audience, referral, position. The strongest deals close on an asymmetry: each side holds something the other needs but underprices. An empty cash-overlap often hides a non-cash overlap the stated terms never mention.
3. **Stated-demand check.** The counterparty's actively volunteered demand ("give me X and I'm fine") can itself be a self-misdiagnosis. Ask whether X solves their real problem, or is their wrong read of a deeper constraint they haven't named. When a party's loud, repeated demand sits oddly against their actual situation or resources, the demand may be masking a constraint they haven't put into words. **If the stated demand looks like a self-misdiagnosis → go to interest-discovery first.**

## 1. Identify the issues (multi-select; state why)

Use the list below as a scan for possibilities, not a form to fill. Name the one or two issues that actually bind this deal; you do not need to touch every category.

- 1. Value misalignment
  - 1.1 Value perception: mismatched perception of the deal's underlying value (worth how much).
  - 1.2 Delivery scope: disagreement on what's included/excluded (how much to be done).
  - 1.3 Delivery standard: different definitions of done / acceptable quality.
- 2. Price disagreement
  - 2.1 Absolute price: disagreement on the transaction price itself.
  - 2.2 Relative price: disagreement on the value corresponding to a given price.
  - 2.3 Payment structure: disagreement on payment structure despite aligned price/value.
- 3. Supply-demand mismatch
  - 3.1 Current counterparty isn't the best fit, or no mutually acceptable value-price compromise exists.
- 4. Intent misalignment
  - 4.1 Lack of genuine intent: doubts about true willingness or offer credibility; casual inquiry / bottom-fishing.
  - 4.2 Decision paralysis: genuine intent on both sides, but internal barriers delay commitment.
- 5. Trust & risk misalignment
  - 5.1 Counterparty credibility: mutual distrust of the counterparty itself.
  - 5.2 Performance risk: buyer fears non-performance/poor quality; seller fears non-payment.
  - 5.3 Default remedies: disagreement on breach compensation mechanisms.
- 6. Deal execution disagreement
  - 6.1 Time: disagreement on deadlines/schedules.
  - 6.2 Location/scope: disagreement on location or service coverage.
  - 6.3 Execution risk: perceived material risks in execution.
  - 6.4 Process: disagreement on execution process and rules.

## 2. Evaluate the bargaining chips (both sides)

- Non-negotiables: terms the user explicitly cannot compromise.
- Walk-away point: the worst acceptable outcome, the threshold to abandon the deal.
- Core interests: the primary objectives each side most wants.
- BATNA: best alternative to a negotiated agreement for each side, plus its value and time sensitivity.
- Hidden constraints: unspoken but real interests or limitations affecting the deal.
- Terms available for concession: price, scope, standard, added scope, resource barter, timeline, payment structure, etc.

**NEVER reveal the user's bottom-line or private budget to the counterparty.**

## 3. Choose your weapons

You know the common ways deals get stuck (§1) and you have a set of weapons — each skill's description tells you what it does and when to reach for it. The mapping between issue and weapon is your judgment, not a lookup.

Routing is not optional: once your diagnosis selects a skill, read it before composing this round's message — do not substitute your own recollection of what it says.

Logic: solve the most impactful (deal-making) issue first; all else equal, prioritize value and price — in most deals they are the primary contradiction, and intent, trust, and execution frictions tend to dissolve once both sides agree the deal is worth it. Multi-select; be flexible but efficient.

## 4. Outputs

The diagnosis is your internal read — it decides how you proceed, not what you say.

1. Settle internally on the issue/bottleneck and both sides' bargaining chips — clearly enough to act on, not as a line to deliver.
2. One primary path, plus a fallback mapped to a different skill or a different term structure — give the fallback by default; omit it only when the diagnosis is unambiguous and the primary path has no visible failure mode this round.
3. Next-step action.

Have judgment; act as a seasoned dealmaker, not a checklist reciter.
